Week 4 · Relationships between categorical variables

Six interactive experiments to build cross-tabulations, explore direction and strength, and understand statistical significance. Change the data, compare the results, and check your interpretation.

All examples are teaching illustrations. The education, candidate preference and counterinsurgency tables reproduce numerical examples from the Week 4 lecture slides.

1 · From one variable to two

Univariate analysis describes a single variable. Bivariate analysis asks whether knowing the independent variable (X) helps us predict the dependent variable (Y).

Think about it

What makes a relationship positive or negative? Can every pair of categorical variables have a direction?

Nominal categories have no inherent order. Ordinal categories do.

2 · Build and read a cross-tabulation

Compare party identification across two groups. The same counts can tell very different stories depending on the denominator. For the question “How does party identification vary by gender?”, compare column percentages, because gender is in the columns.

3 · Strength and direction: Kendall’s tau-b

When both variables are ordinal, Kendall’s tau-b describes the direction and strength of their association, accounting for tied pairs. Change the pattern of political interest across education levels.

Kendall’s tau-b

Concordant pairs increase together; discordant pairs move in opposite directions. Pairs tied on either variable affect the denominator.

4 · Lambda: does knowing X improve predictions?

For a nominal dependent variable, lambda measures the proportional reduction in prediction errors when we use the independent variable. This example follows the lecture’s 2004 Democratic primary vote table.

Prediction errors

Lambda = (errors without X − errors with X) ÷ errors without X.

Knowledge check: Can lambda equal zero even if the group distributions differ?
Choose an answer.

5 · Statistical independence: observed versus expected

Under the null hypothesis of independence, the expected count in each cell is (row total × column total) ÷ N. Compare the lecture’s counterinsurgency campaign counts to what independence would predict.

Example: expected losses at mechanisation level 1 = 60 × 45 ÷ 167 = 16.17, versus 12 observed.

Knowledge check: If X and Y are independent, what should we expect?
Choose an answer.

6 · Chi-square: strength of evidence, not strength of association

Start with the lecture’s counterinsurgency table, then vary sample size and association intensity. See how observed counts diverge from expected counts and how the Pearson chi-square test responds. This is a deterministic simulation using proportionally scaled counts.

Pearson χ² = Σ (observed − expected)² / expected. Degrees of freedom = (rows − 1) × (columns − 1). We use a 5% threshold for this exercise. A small p-value is evidence against independence, not proof of causation or a measure of effect size.
